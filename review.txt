There is a strong game here. The dungeon design is actually more distinctive than the story at the moment. The best improvement would be to make the **story, characters, treasures, and dungeon verbs all express the same underlying idea**, rather than having a good mechanical game wrapped in a collection of Chinese-mythology references.

One other point first: if "the four Chinese classic novels" is meant literally, the current design is missing *Dream of the Red Chamber*. The conventional Four Great Classical Novels are *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, *Water Margin*, *Journey to the West*, and *Dream of the Red Chamber*. ([Visit Beijing][1]) That actually gives you a very useful missing theme: **dream/reality, illusion, impermanence, and the decline of worldly things**. Your Fox Palace is almost tailor-made for it.

## 1. I'd change the premise slightly

Currently:

> The Eight Trigrams Furnace has spilled; its treasures have scattered and the creatures that seized them are demon lords. Zhong Kui sends mortals to recover them.

This gives you a reason to visit dungeons, but not really a *story*.

I'd make the Furnace's contents more important:

> **The Eight Trigrams Furnace does not merely forge weapons. It refines things that do not belong together: mortal and immortal, spirit and flesh, fire and metal, dream and waking.**
>
> Something has gone wrong. The Furnace has broken its seal, and the things it had consumed have returned to the world in corrupted forms. Demons have taken the Furnace's treasures and made themselves lords of the regions around them.
>
> Zhong Kui cannot enter the Furnace himself. The laws of Heaven forbid an immortal from retrieving what Heaven has lost.
>
> So he recruits four mortals.

That gives the mortals a reason to exist.

More importantly, I'd make the **treasures pieces of the Furnace's operating principle**, rather than random magical items.

For example:

| Treasure      | What it represents | Mechanical idea                        |
| ------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| Gourd         | **Containment**    | store/transfer things                  |
| Jade Vase     | **Separation**     | keep multiple substances/objects apart |
| Diamond Snare | **Binding**        | connect things that are distant        |
| Iron Fan      | **Direction**      | redirect forces                        |
| Mirror        | **Revelation**     | distinguish true/false                 |
| Bell          | **Command**        | impose order on spirits                |
| Final Furnace | **Transformation** | combine everything                     |

Then the game has an almost mathematical progression:

**contain → separate → bind → redirect → reveal → command → transform**

And the finale can actually depend on what the player has learned.

# 2. The biggest story improvement: the Furnace wasn't simply "spilled"

I'd avoid eventually revealing:

> "Oh, the fox did it."

That's functional but predictable.

Instead, I'd make the answer somewhat stranger:

### The Furnace was opened deliberately — but not by a villain.

Perhaps **someone wanted something inside it to escape**.

The Furnace has been used for ages to refine demons, spirits, weapons, elixirs, and immortals. One of the things imprisoned inside has discovered that the Furnace is not destroying things.

It is **changing them**.

And something has been trying to get out.

This lets the finale recontextualize the whole game.

The monsters aren't necessarily just thieves who stole treasures. Some of them may have **chosen to take them because they understood what the Furnace was doing**.

The Golden and Silver Horned Kings aren't merely greedy bosses.

Red Boy isn't merely sitting on fire.

The Hundred-Eyed Demon isn't merely guarding a mirror.

Each has taken something because **it gives them a piece of freedom from whatever the Furnace does**.

Then the player gradually has to decide:

> Are we actually repairing Heaven's mistake?

That is a much more interesting question than "collect all the artifacts."

# 3. Give Zhong Kui a more interesting role

Zhong Kui is an excellent choice because he is already a supernatural demon-queller, but I wouldn't make him the generic quest-giver.

Make him **the person who knows more than he tells you**.

For example, at the beginning:

> "Recover the treasures. Do not enter the Furnace."

Then later:

> "Do not trust the Fox."

And eventually:

> "I did not tell you what was inside because you would have refused to go."

He doesn't have to be evil. He could be caught between **Heaven's order and the mortals' interests**.

That also gives you a nice recurring character who can have dry, slightly absurd interactions with the four heroes.

The tone of *Journey to the West* is useful here: enormous supernatural stakes combined with occasional comedy and ridiculous personalities. That contrast is part of what makes the mythology work. ([China-US Focus][2])

# 4. Your four heroes should be characters, not four weapon loadouts

I'd make them **four different answers to the question "how should a mortal confront the supernatural?"**

Something like:

### The General — glaive

**Role:** reach, defense, posture.

Personality:

* disciplined
* formal
* believes in duty
* initially trusts Zhong Kui completely

Gameplay:

* longest melee reach
* strongest parry/block
* excellent at controlling space
* relatively slow
* perhaps weakest at recovering after mistakes

He is the obvious Three Kingdoms archetype.

But don't make him literally Guan Yu unless you want to. A fictional hero *resembling* Guan Yu gives you much more freedom.

---

### The Outlaw — dao

This is where I'd deliberately bring in *Water Margin*.

**Role:** mobility, flanking, finishing.

Personality:

* irreverent
* practical
* suspicious of Heaven's bureaucracy
* perhaps has killed a tiger or two

Gameplay:

* dodge
* back attacks
* quick posture damage
* climbing/mobility
* weaker frontal defense

He can be the character who repeatedly asks:

> "Why exactly are we helping Heaven?"

That becomes important later.

---

### The Archer

**Role:** distance, precision, environmental interaction.

Personality:

* quiet
* observant
* practical
* notices things the others miss

Gameplay:

* shoots switches/ropes/eyes/fire sources
* strongest ranged damage
* weak when surrounded
* can exploit verticality

Don't just make the archer "the ranged DPS." Make **the bow another way of solving the dungeon**.

For example, in Bamboo Valley:

* shoot a rope to drop a burning bundle
* shoot a snake before it reaches the party
* ignite something from a distance
* shoot an enemy's exposed weak point

The bow becomes part of the puzzle language.

---

### The Scholar / Taoist

This should probably be your most interesting character.

**Role:** manipulation, spirits, knowledge.

Weapon:

* folding fan initially
* wooden jian later, perhaps as a secondary stance

Personality:

* educated
* curious
* slightly arrogant
* understands that the supernatural follows rules

He should be the one who understands what the treasures actually are.

And I would make him **not necessarily a wizard**.

He's a scholar who knows ritual, geometry, names, seals, astrology, Taoist practices, etc.

So instead of:

> "mage casts spell"

you get:

> "scholar understands the rule and exploits it."

That fits your existing design extremely well.

# 5. Give each character a flaw that the story actually uses

This is more important than giving them elaborate backstories.

| Character | Strength      | Blind spot                              |
| --------- | ------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| General   | Duty          | Obeys authority too readily             |
| Outlaw    | Independence  | Distrusts authority even when justified |
| Archer    | Observation   | Hesitates to commit                     |
| Scholar   | Understanding | Thinks understanding means control      |

Then the Furnace can force them to confront those assumptions.

That's much better than four characters who merely have different attacks.

And it gives you natural dialogue.

The General:

> "Zhong Kui gave us our orders."

The Outlaw:

> "And Heaven gave the demon his orders."

The Scholar:

> "Both statements can be true."

The Archer:

> "That's not reassuring."

That sort of exchange would establish the game's tone very quickly.

# 6. The four novels can become four *flavors* of the game

This is where I think your concept can become considerably stronger.

The four classics aren't just sources for monsters. Each can supply a different narrative mode.

### Journey to the West → the supernatural world

This is your:

* demons
* immortals
* magical objects
* absurd bosses
* transformations
* Zhong Kui
* Furnace

That's already working.

### Water Margin → the heroes

The heroes don't have to be obedient fantasy adventurers. They can have the **outlaw camaraderie and suspicion of official authority** associated with *Water Margin*. The novel is heavily concerned with fellowship, resistance, violence, and the ambiguity of outlaw justice. ([China-US Focus][2])

### Romance of the Three Kingdoms → the four protagonists

Their personalities, rivalries, tactical thinking, military aesthetics and weapons can evoke the Three Kingdoms tradition.

### Dream of the Red Chamber → the Fox Palace / ending

This is the missing piece.

The Fox Spirit doesn't merely create illusions.

She creates **beautiful things that aren't real**.

The Palace gradually becomes more attractive as it becomes less real.

Perhaps:

* a ruined courtyard becomes a magnificent palace
* dead NPCs appear alive
* defeated bosses appear again
* a dead parent appears
* a lost lover appears
* the heroes see themselves as great generals
* Zhong Kui appears and tells them they have already won

And the mirror doesn't merely reveal invisible walls.

It reveals:

> **what the world actually is.**

That's a much more meaningful use of your mirror.

*Dream of the Red Chamber* is particularly concerned with the relationship between dream and reality and the transience of worldly things, so this isn't merely bolting the fourth novel onto the game. ([Wikipedia][3])

# 7. I'd change the Fox Palace considerably

Right now:

> Corridors loop, doors lead back where you came from, walls are false; the mirror shows the true layout.

Good puzzle.

But it should be the **emotional climax**, not merely the "illusion dungeon."

I'd make the player progressively unsure whether the earlier game was real.

For example:

### Stage 1 — Spatial illusion

Walls and doors lie.

### Stage 2 — Enemy illusion

Enemies aren't necessarily what they appear to be.

### Stage 3 — Character illusion

A party member appears somewhere they aren't.

### Stage 4 — Narrative illusion

NPCs tell contradictory versions of what happened.

### Stage 5 — Personal illusion

Each hero is shown something they want.

### Final boss

The Fox becomes one of the heroes.

Not:

> "Which one is the fox?"

but:

> **"Which version of this character is real?"**

And the answer isn't determined by appearance. It is determined by behavior/mechanics.

That would make your **purple warning = don't act** suddenly acquire narrative significance.

The player has been trained for hours to recognize:

> purple = wait / observe / don't trust what you're seeing.

Now the final dungeon makes that a philosophical mechanic.

# 10. Your enemy design principle is excellent — make it explicit

This:

> **Each enemy family forces a different response and checks a different class**

is one of the strongest ideas in the document.

I'd formalize every enemy with four questions:

1. **What does it make you do?**
2. **Which character does that favor?**
3. **What mistake does it punish?**
4. **What environmental interaction can defeat it?**

For example:

### Scorpion

> Front → bad
> Side/rear → good
> Claw → dodge
> Sting → parry
> Fan → environmental displacement
> Snare → expose flank

Now the enemy isn't just "a scorpion with attacks."

It participates in your game's vocabulary.

You don't need a BioShock-style moral-choice tree.

At the Furnace:

The scholar says:

> "And the creatures?"

> "They will be returned to their proper places."

> "And if their proper place is the Furnace?"

Return everything to Heaven's order.

or

### Break it

Destroy the mechanism that decides what belongs where.

### Enter it

The heroes themselves become the final ingredients.

That gives you a proper thematic conclusion to:

**containment → binding → control → revelation → transformation.**

# 2. The four heroes can have a very clean personality architecture

I'd actually stop thinking of them primarily as Guan Yu / Zhuge Liang / etc. Those are their **cultural archetypes**.

Their interpersonal archetypes can be Western / universal.

Something like:

| Hero    | Chinese ancestry           | Universal archetype | Group role                |
| ------- | -------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Glaive  | Guan Yu / Sha Wujing       | **The Guardian**    | conscience / protector    |
| Dao     | Wukong / Water Margin      | **The Rebel**       | initiative / troublemaker |
| Bow     | hunters / Water Margin     | **The Wanderer**    | observer / pragmatist     |
| Scholar | Zhuge Liang / Tang Sanzang | **The Sage**        | interpreter / strategist  |

That gives you four very legible personalities without making them stereotypes.

And importantly, they don't need to get along.

# 3. The Odyssey is probably the most useful Western model

Not because you should copy Odysseus, but because *The Odyssey* has a structure remarkably suited to your game.

It's fundamentally:

> **A man travels through a world of supernatural obstacles trying to return home.**

Your game could initially appear to be:

> Four heroes travel through supernatural lands collecting treasures.

But gradually the question becomes:

> **Where are they actually going home to?**

That becomes especially interesting if the heroes don't all have the same home.

The General wants to return to his family/duty.

The Outlaw doesn't really have a home.

The Archer wants somewhere quiet.

The Scholar wants answers.

So "return home" means something different to each of them.

And the Fox Palace becomes your **Circe/Calypso/Odyssey territory**: a place that offers the heroes exactly what they want, and therefore threatens to keep them there.

---

# 4. Childe Harold gives you another useful character type

Byron's Childe Harold is quite different from the heroic-adventure model.

He's the **disillusioned wanderer**.

He travels because he is dissatisfied with the world he came from.

That could be an excellent basis for your archer.

He's not an outlaw and not a hero seeking glory.

He's simply someone who has become alienated from ordinary society and prefers the wilderness.

His character arc isn't:

> "I must become a hero."

It's:

> **"I have to decide whether I actually belong anywhere."**

That would give the party some melancholy beneath the comedy and adventure.

And *Childe Harold* also gives you the Romantic fascination with ruins, nature, antiquity, and the sublime. Your Tomb City and Flaming Mountains could carry that atmosphere without explicitly referencing Byron.

---

# 5. The Hero's Journey is useful — but I'd avoid following it too literally

You already have:

**ordinary world → call → supernatural threshold → trials → ordeal → revelation → return**

But your game is particularly well suited to making the Hero's Journey **apply to the party rather than an individual**.

The four heroes collectively form the hero.

That solves a problem inherent in a four-character game.

Instead of:

> "Who is the protagonist?"

the answer is:

> **The party is the protagonist.**

Each character undergoes a different piece of the journey.

For example:

### Reed Marsh

**Call to adventure**

"Recover the Furnace treasures."

### Bamboo / Mountains / Caverns

**Tests**

The party learns to operate as a group.

### Tomb City

**Descent**

They enter the world of the dead.

### Fox Palace

**Abyss / temptation**

They confront false versions of themselves.

### Furnace

**Revelation**

They discover why they were chosen.

That's a very clean structure.

---

# 6. I'd add one more Western archetype: the Argonauts

This might actually fit even better than the Hero's Journey.

The Argonauts are a **team of specialized heroes**, each with different abilities, undertaking a dangerous quest for an extraordinary object.

That's almost exactly your party.

And importantly, **Jason isn't necessarily the most capable person in the group**. The expedition works because different people contribute different capabilities.

That matches your dungeon philosophy:

> No character should be able to solve everything optimally.

The party itself becomes the machine.

---

# 7. And perhaps The Fellowship of the Ring — but only structurally

Again, not visually.

The useful thing about Tolkien is the **fellowship as a temporary alliance of fundamentally different people**.

You could have:

* the honorable warrior
* the irreverent outsider
* the wilderness specialist
* the scholar

They aren't friends at the beginning.

They become friends because **they survive things together**.

This is important because I'd avoid the modern tendency to have everyone immediately banter like lifelong friends.

Let them be awkward together.

Early:

> **General:** "You are going the wrong way."

> **Outlaw:** "There are no roads."

> **General:** "Exactly."

Later:

> **Outlaw:** "You know, you're getting better at going the wrong way."

> **General:** "I learned from you."

That makes the relationship earned.

---

# 8. The Master Splinter idea suggests something else: a fifth character who isn't playable

I'd strongly consider giving the party a **small companion**.

Not a combatant.

Something like:

* a little spirit
* a fox cub
* a crow
* a furnace imp
* a tiny peach demon
* perhaps even a transformed fragment of the Furnace

It accompanies the party and provides commentary.

But unlike a conventional mascot, it should **change its relationship to the heroes**.

Initially:

> "Zhong Kui told me to watch you."

Later:

> "I was told to watch you. I wasn't told I would like you."

Eventually:

> "I'm coming with you."

That gives you your Splinter/TMNT connective tissue without turning Zhong Kui into a direct copy.

---

# 9. There's also an interesting Western archetype you haven't mentioned: Don Quixote

This could be useful for one of the characters, particularly the scholar.

Don Quixote is someone who **interprets reality through stories**.

That's almost the inverse of your Fox Spirit.

The Fox says:

> "Reality can look like anything."

The Don Quixote archetype says:

> "Reality is whatever story I believe I'm living."

Your scholar could therefore have a subtle weakness:

**he trusts texts too much.**

He knows the legends.

He knows the rules.

He knows what demons are *supposed* to do.

And then the actual world repeatedly violates his expectations.

That would be a nice way to stop Zhuge Liang from becoming "the guy who is always right."

---

# 10. Another useful archetype: the trickster

You already have one almost automatically in the Wukong/outlaw.

But I'd make the distinction:

**The Scholar believes the world has rules.**

**The Outlaw discovers that rules have loopholes.**

That gives them a genuinely complementary relationship.

The scholar figures out:

> "The demon cannot cross running water."

The outlaw figures out:

> "But it can throw things across it."

The scholar:

> "I hadn't considered that."

The outlaw:

> "That's why you keep me around."

This is also exactly what you want mechanically: **one character discovers the rule; another exploits the loophole.**

---

# 11. You could quietly borrow the "four temperaments" as well

Not necessarily explicitly.

Something like:

### Glaive

**Duty**

"Someone has to stand here."

### Dao

**Desire**

"I want to know what's over there."

### Bow

**Experience**

"That looks like a trap."

### Scholar

**Reason**

"There is a pattern."

Those are four different ways of approaching the unknown.

And then the game's philosophy becomes:

> **Duty without curiosity becomes obedience.
> Curiosity without discipline becomes recklessness.
> Experience without reason becomes superstition.
> Reason without experience becomes abstraction.**

None of the four is sufficient.

That's a surprisingly good justification for why the party needs four people.

---

# 12. I'd make the character arcs converge at the Furnace

This is where all these archetypes become worthwhile.

At the beginning:

### General

> "We have a duty."

### Outlaw

> "We don't owe Heaven anything."

### Archer

> "Let's just survive."

### Scholar

> "We need to understand what happened."

At the Furnace, each has changed.

The General has learned that **authority can be wrong**.

The Outlaw has learned that **freedom requires responsibility**.

The Archer has discovered **something worth belonging to**.

The Scholar has learned that **understanding does not imply control**.

And then the final question isn't simply:

> "What do we do with the Furnace?"

It's:

> **"Who are we now that we have reached it?"**

That gives the game a real coming-of-age structure without requiring the characters to be children.

---

## The overall synthesis I'd aim for

The game could quietly contain all of these layers:

**Chinese literary layer:**
*Journey to the West* + *Water Margin* + *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* + *Dream of the Red Chamber*

**Mythological layer:**
Zhong Kui, Laozi, demons, immortals, Furnace, peaches

**Western epic layer:**
*The Odyssey* + Argonauts + Hero's Journey

**Romantic layer:**
*Childe Harold* — wandering, alienation, sublime landscapes

**Character/adventure layer:**
Fellowship + found family + Master/disciples

**Trickster layer:**
Sun Wukong / outlaw / Don Quixote

**Mechanical layer:**
Each character embodies a different way of interacting with the world.

And none of that needs to be explained to the player.

Ideally, the player simply experiences:

> *These four people are very different. They keep arguing. Somehow they work extremely well together. I know why each one is here. And by the end, I care what happens to all four.*

That's probably the strongest version of the concept.
